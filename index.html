Every story starts with the country and the place: the country so you know which part of the world it is, the place so you know where the mark is standing. A story that named a country and not a town is pinned at that country's capital, and the second name is the capital rather than the scene. What it is about is guessed the same way as the mood, from words — switch the kinds you want and it is remembered, so the next time you open it the world arrives already filtered. Good or bad is guessed here in the page, from a word list of about three hundred words a news line actually uses. Every free sentiment service refuses a browser, so there is no API to call. It cannot read irony and it counts a rescue after a disaster twice; it is good for the shape of a day, not for one story. Only from far out — zoom past the whole world and it all goes away, and they sit in two rails, one each side of the map, whichever half of the globe each one is on, on lines back to their own places. They grow and shrink with the zoom. Put the pointer on one and the words walk along until the end of the sentence; click and the story opens at its source. and the wordmark at the top brings you back. Two free sources, no key and no account: Wikipedia's Current events portal for today and yesterday, and the USGS for earthquakes. A story is put on the map by the first capital or country its own words name, which is a guess and sometimes the wrong one; earthquakes carry their own coordinates and are exact. Click one to read it at the source. Kept for 24 hours in this browser and nowhere else.

PAC-MAN, ON THE REAL STREETS NOT PLAYING SCORE 0 DOTS LEFT — LIVES — GO TO — SPEED — Montpellier, from above, and only from close enough to see the streets — zoom out past the city and the game stops rather than floating over a continent. Take the dots, keep away from the four, and get to wherever GO TO is pointing — round the corner, or twelve thousand kilometres away. The orange ones are worth more and make you faster, 5% then 10% then 100%. Nothing walks through a building, but a jump clears one, and a long press jumps further than a tap. Caught, you come apart into your own pixels and start again where you started.

SCALE TRUE — REAL PROPORTIONS SHOWING — At true scale everything keeps its real proportions: the Sun is half a degree wide from here, the Moon is sixty Earth radii away, and the planets are points of light, because that is what they are. Squashed scale pulls the distances in so the whole system fits one screen — readable, and a diagram. Sizes, masses, orbits and spins from the NASA planetary fact sheet; the three angles that say where each planet sits on its orbit at J2000 from Wikipedia. THE WHOLE STORY

Everything, from the beginning to the year on the bar. The deep figures are rounded consensus estimates — each one links to its article, so you can check it rather than take my word. The years from 1500 come from Wikidata, ranked by how many Wikipedia editions carry the event.

THIS YEAR

Events are what Wikidata classes as an event — a war, a treaty, an election, an eruption, a spaceflight, a pandemic — ranked by coverage. Something famous that Wikidata has not typed as an event will not be here, however big it was. That is a limit of the source, not a judgement.
